AN IMMERSIVE CASE STUDY EXERCISE

Two Sessions: Alicia

You are the therapist. Alicia, late twenties, came home with one requirement left on her degree: the thesis. Two sessions, a week apart, and what you ask of her in between will shape what she brings back.

Two gauges track her: Pressure, how close she is to freezing and postponing, with a workable band marked on the bar, and Trust, how safe she feels with you. Too much pressure and she stalls completely. Too little, and nothing changes. There is no perfect script.

Reading your trace

  • Where did pressure climb fastest, and what standard of hers did that touch?
  • Which choices felt supportive but changed nothing, the way her parents' reassurance does?
  • What did the size of your step teach her, whether or not she wrote anything?

Take it to your group

Before anything else: choose one detail from Alicia's story that you find important or puzzling, and invite each person in your group to share theirs.

Your task: build an interpretation of what keeps Alicia stuck and explore what might help. Several interpretations may fit, so use details from the case to explain yours. Start with a question that interests your group. You don't need to finish every question or agree with each other.

  • Alicia wants to do excellent work. Why isn't that getting her started? What does she seem motivated to achieve, versus what might she be motivated to avoid? Could caring more about the result make starting harder, and under what conditions might it instead help her persist?
  • By the end of another week without a draft, what might Alicia have learned? Pick one repeated sequence (planning instead of writing, postponing a start, receiving reassurance, deciding the day is already lost) and trace what happens right after it, and what that might teach her about the task, herself, or other people.
  • “Just write a bad first draft.” Why might sensible advice be hard to use? What might a classmate mean by it, and what might Alicia hear instead? What would have to change for a rough draft to feel like progress? Two students get the same harsh feedback, one revises and one stops; what could explain the difference, and might it apply here?
  • Her advisors ask: more structure, or more freedom? Pick one concrete change, say what you hope it would shift in her experience of the work, and how it could backfire. What would tell you it was helping her become more independent, rather than just more compliant?

When you have an initial answer, stay with it a little longer:

  • What in the story makes us think that?
  • What else could explain the same behavior?
  • What would we need to know to tell the difference?

For example, “she lacks motivation” is a starting point. Does she not want the outcome, doubt she can achieve it, find the process hard to tolerate, or see no manageable way to begin? Would those possibilities call for the same response? She has also declined an evaluation for depression or ADHD, so it's worth asking what else, beyond a personality style, could be shaping what you're seeing, and what you'd need to know to tell the difference.

Changing your mind, questioning an interpretation, and noticing missing information all count as contributions. You don't need to share personal experiences. Please discuss without AI. No written answers are required. Bring back to class, if you can: one interpretation your group developed, a concept that helped explain it, and one question you still can't answer.
